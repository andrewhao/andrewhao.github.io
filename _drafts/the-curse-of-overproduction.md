The Curse of Overproduction by Andrew Hao

As AI speeds up everything about knowledge work, we hear claims that AI will produce 100x engineers or people. The output of these people will be so voluminous because they'll have swarms of agents working for them. We'll have a single person able to manage the output of a hundred, the equivalent of hundreds of workers. I find this highly, I am highly skeptical of this claim, and yet I also can see the increasing pressure that it is creating upon us all in our knowledge working fields.

Now, myself as an engineer, this is very common. This is very likely simply because of how things are sped up. In my early career, I was a manager, and being a manager meant stepping away from the work and letting other people do the work for me. In my work, very much became planning the work, reviewing the work, inspecting, and managing the system. Much has been written about how a lot of this work is really going to befall management, and while this is very, very seductive, it's also incredibly unsatisfying. As a manager, I found myself actually coming to work early because I really enjoyed the craft of production and the craft of coding. Now that's a separate topic, but let's get back to the question about AI swarms in the high tempo of everyday life.

We see that now small teams are able to accomplish what formerly required large teams, and I 100% agree with this. I think small two-person, ten-person startups are going to accomplish what orgs you know twice or five times your size are doing, but I think this will come at the cost of the people or the workers managing the work with every context, which comes with a lot of cognitive burden. It's going to be a lot of burnout. There's a lot of merely the act of reading and reviewing the code or the output or the artifacts, which is alone mentally draining and very taxing. The tempo of life under these AI agents we don't believe is going to get any slower. In many ways, this is kind of like the technological advancements since the beginning of time, from the industrial revolution on to the knowledge revolution. There has been a continual ratcheting up, or the boiling of the frog. Life has never freed us up to become more relaxed. It's never freed up more time. Instead, we've learned just to pack even more stuff into it.

This is my worry about our involvement in machine-driven systems or automated agent agentic systems: whether the tempo of life will continue to barrel ahead, but the core essence of humanity, our need to work and rest and play, is going to be drowned out by the continuous drumbeat of progress or competition. On a very personal note, I've been spending hours every evening working on projects enabled by AI, studying new advances coming out daily, reading and reviewing the work generated by the open-source community. Quite frankly, it's very exciting with it, but I realized to myself the other week that a lot of this was driven by the fear of becoming obsolete, of missing out on the wave, or of being discarded by an organization that may find me unnecessary or obsolete. I certainly don't want that, right, so I'm kind of upskilling myself.

In a world where overproduction or constant production is the norm, where does the human have time to step away? As machines continue to automate and optimize, where does the human get to step back and say, "This is enough. I don't need to sub-linearly scale myself. My company finds my contribution enough"? Where does the boss or the worker or the manager of humans look over their humans and say, "This is the right team. They are producing enough"? As we see the technological-driven pressure crank up even more than it has been before, it's hard to see a world where there will be enough.
